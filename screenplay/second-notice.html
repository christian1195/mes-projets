SECOND NOTICE
A screenplay
Adapted from the novella Second Notice
Running time: approx. 40 minutes

THE LOOK AND THE SOUND

– Silence is the monster's breath. No wind, no insects, no birds. When it is near, the room tone drops out of the mix. The first mosquito, at the very end, is the loveliest sound in the film.

– The knocks are the score. One is a greeting. Two is a count. Three is a verdict. Under them, a sub-bass tone at the pitch of a bell not yet struck. No orchestral music until the river.

– We never see all of it until the fire. A hand. A shoulder. A branch snapping at nine feet. Keep the left third of the frame dark.

– The flashlight is a character. When it clicks off, the screen goes fully black.

– Time is audible. Dog's watch ticks under the mix whenever his wrist is in frame. It stops at 3:40.

FADE IN:

BLACK.

HOLD on black, longer than is comfortable. A diesel engine ticks as it cools. Chains settle on a load. And we hear what is missing: no wind, no night birds, no insects.

Under it, barely there, one sub-bass tone: a bell in the instant before it is struck. It does not resolve.

From far off, wood on wood. One KNOCK. Round, polite, like a knuckle on a door.

SUPER: ALASKA. LATE JULY, 1975.

EXT. THE GRADE – NIGHT

A gouge of packed gravel cut through white spruce. A gray dusk that will never get darker.

A loaded logging truck sits dead across the road, one headlight throwing a weak yellow cone. HIGH WIDE, long lens: the truck is a toy on a brown thread laid across a huge green-black field. The spruce stand in ranks like an audience that has stopped talking.

Far back among the trunks, one is thicker and grayer than the rest, standing slightly apart. We won't notice it. We'll remember it later.

2.

SUPER: 12:14 AM

JIM (25, skinny as a coat hanger, scraggly beard, a knit cap pulled down over his ears, a grin you would lend money to) paces the running board. Hops down. Paces the gravel. Swings the cab door open and shut. He talks the way other people sweat.

JIM

It's the alternator. No. The battery. No, it's Walt. Walt rode in this truck in June and thought, what's the single worst thing I can do to Jim's July? And here we are. A man. A truck. A dusk that won't quit.

He smacks the fender.

JIM (CONT'D)

Pipeline camps pay double on anything scaled before six. Somewhere out there a man is cashing my double right now. I can feel it leaving. Like a draft.

DOG (34, Black, tall, broad through the shoulders, big scarred hands) stands at the front tire with his hands in his jacket. He has been awake nineteen hours. He lets Jim's talk go past him like weather.

DOG

Check the fuses.

JIM

I checked the fuses. I made peace with the fuses. The fuses and I are fine.

Dog walks a slow circle around the cab. Through the window, behind the seat, a .30-06 hangs in a rack. Dog's eyes slide past it like it is a stranger on a bus.

He stops. Looks at the sky. At the trees. Nothing moves. Not a branch, not a needle, not one mosquito.

JIM

Where are the mosquitoes? I was promised mosquitoes. It's the one thing this state has going for it.

DOG

I know.

3.

HOLD on Dog. Five full seconds. Then the camera drifts off him toward the tree line and settles on the black between two trunks. Stays there.

KNOCK. From the spruce. One. Wood on wood. It rolls across the clearing and fades.

Dog's shoulders go tight. Jim's grin goes wide. His knuckles are already up. He raps the cab door once: sharp, polite, neighborly.

JIM

Evening!

DOG

Quiet.

JIM

Every July. Same as last July. It's the payroll.

He holds up his left hand. On the palm, ballpoint ink, years old and re-inked so many times it has gone fuzzy: KNOCK BACK. TOLL. WHOLE?

JIM (CONT'D)

Pruitt's rules. First week on the grade, he made me write them on my hand like a kid. One, they knock, you knock back. Two, you leave the toll at the ford. Three, it's gotta be whole. Whole what? Pruitt never said. Pruitt said a lot of things, mostly to a jukebox.

DOG

Nothing's free out here.

JIM

Everything's free out here. That's why they send us.

He waits, grinning, for the second hello. The woods always give one more. It's the best part of the bit.

The answer comes. It is not a hello.

KNOCK. KNOCK.

Two. Slow, spaced, deliberate. A count. It doesn't roll across the clearing. It arrives.

Jim's grin holds half a second too long.

4.

JIM (CONT'D)

Huh. That's new. Is that, like, a regional thing? An accent?

Dog takes his hands out of his pockets. He walks to the edge of the headlight's cone. A rotted stump at the shoulder of the road, knee-high, a white X spray-painted on its face. Dog's hand drifts down to his right boot, where a small leather POUCH rides against his ankle.

INSERT – THE YARD – DAY (JUNE)

Sunlight on gravel. A line of trucks. WALT (50s, short, square, pencil behind his ear) hands Jim a thick envelope. Block capitals across the flap, underlined twice: TOLL.

Jim folds it into his shirt pocket without looking at it. Grinning.

Thirty feet off, at the corner of a trailer, Dog watches him do it. Then looks at the sky.

BACK TO SCENE

DOG

Get in the cab.

JIM

You can't make me get in the cab for saying hello. They started it.

DOG

Jim.

Flat. Short. Nobody has ever said his name to him like that. Jim's face does something. He climbs into the cab.

INT. TRUCK CAB – CONTINUOUS

Jim taps the dash, nervous. Once, twice. Once, twice. The woods do not answer that. He talks to Dog through the gap in the window.

JIM

Scale of one to ten. Fear. I'm a six, but a loud six. Your turn.

DOG (O.S.)

Four.

5.

JIM

Four! Four is a man waiting for a bus.

No answer. Jim grabs the CB mic, cord coiled like something asleep.

JIM (CONT'D)

Breaker one-nine, this is Jim, the world's unluckiest logger, calling Walt. Calling a priest. Calling anybody with a sandwich.

The hiss stops. Not fades. Stops, as if a hand has been laid over a mouth. The cab's room tone goes with it: the tick of the engine, the creak of the seat, Jim's own breath. Total vacuum. For one second we hear the pulse in his ear.

VOICE (CB)

Jim.

He laughs, one beat early.

JIM

Walt? If this is your idea of customer service...

VOICE (CB)

Jim.

Same pitch. Same pace. The horrible exactness of a tape of his own voice played back. Jim hangs up the mic gently, like something that might go off. His eyes flick to the rearview. The road. The trees. Nothing.

JIM

Radios get ghosts. It's a known thing. Dog, I want it on the record that I'm taking this extremely well.

Through the windshield, Dog is staring at him. Not afraid. Staring at him like a man staring at a clock.

DOG

Out.

JIM

Now? Two minutes ago you ordered me in...

DOG

Jim. Out.

6.

The headlight DIPS. The beam slides down the gravel as the front of the truck rises six inches off the road.

SOUND: the bell tone returns, climbing. Coins on the dash shiver toward the edge. A ring of dust lifts off the hood in a perfect circle. The thermos in the footwell ripples in concentric rings.

DOG'S POV: the bumper lifting slowly, like a drawbridge. The chained load groans and slides. Behind the truck, the spruce are perfectly, obscenely still.

JIM

(small)

Dog.

Dog dives left into the ditch.

The truck goes over. We never see what lifts it. We see the long, slow turn of the truck in the headlight's last swing across the trees, the windshield blooming white. Then the impact, enormous. Then a hard cut to nothing: three seconds of black and total silence.

SMASH TO BLACK.

EXT. THE GRADE – WRECK – NIGHT

SUPER: 12:36 AM

A high flat WHINE, like a saw spinning down. Dog lies in the ditch with gravel in his mouth. Above him a wheel turns in the air, around and around. The picture swims, soft, as if underwater.

His hands lie in front of his face, shaking. He tells them to stop. They don't.

His hearing returns, and with it Jim, screaming his name.

Dog gets up in the wrong order, knees before hands. Goes down. Says to himself:

DOG

Up.

He gets up. The truck lies on its side across the grade, the load groaning on its chains. Dog climbs the fender and hauls the jammed passenger door open. Below him in the dark, Jim looks up, white-faced, lip split.

JIM

It picked it up, Dog.

7.

DOG

I know. Breathe.

JIM

My leg. Get it off, get it...

The gun rack has folded across Jim's left knee. The rifle is still in it.

Dog puts a hand on the stock. Cold, like a stone pulled from a river. He hesitates. Then he pulls. The bracket gives. He works the bolt without deciding to: four rounds in the magazine. The magazine holds five. He closes the bolt and does not look at the empty space.

He slides a tire iron under the last bracket and leans. Jim screams one long word. Dog drags him out.

Jim sits on the door skin with his knee swelling like a loaf. He is sick over the side. Dog holds him by the collar.

JIM

(shivering)

It was a tree. A tree came down on the front end. Trees do that. A moose. They get unbelievably big.

DOG

Jim.

JIM

Okay. Okay. I know.

Dog gets down. On the cab's flank, six inches from his face: a HANDPRINT, driven deep into the dust and diesel above the wheel well. Five fingers spread, the palm wider than his own whole hand. The steel is dented under it. A bead of diesel runs down one finger and falls. We HEAR it land, absurdly loud.

Dog's own hand rises, trembling, and hovers beside it. Tiny.

He turns, finds a diesel can in the ditch, a long flashlight, and pushes the tire iron into Jim's fist. Jim looks at it.

JIM

A crutch. You brought me a crutch. I'm going to name it.

DOG

Walk.

8.

EXT. THE GRADE – ROAD – NIGHT

They go down the road. Dog carries the can and the rifle. Jim hops on the iron, swearing in a high thin voice.

Behind them a binder CRACKS like a gunshot. The whole load comes off the trailer in a grinding thunder and rolls into a wall of logs across the road. The way back is closed.

Three hundred yards on, a spruce lies across the road. Fresh. The break is bright and clean, nine feet off the ground. Dog boosts Jim over. Forty yards on: another. Another. A fence of fallen trees, as far as Dog's flashlight can reach. The beam finds them one at a time, each snapping out of the dark as it arrives, like the night is being switched on in sections.

The stumps stand snapped at nine feet. No axe marks.

JIM

Beavers. Very ambitious beavers.

He sits on the third trunk, laughing, high and awful.

JIM (CONT'D)

I'd like to speak to the beavers' manager.

Behind them, a long way back: KNOCK. KNOCK. Closer than it should be.

Dog looks the other way, down the road behind them. A gap in the spruce. A blaze cut in a tree. A coffee can hanging from a wire like a lantern someone forgot to light. A footpath into the woods.

DOG

We passed it.

JIM

Passed what?

DOG

The path. To the ford.

JIM

No. We are not walking into the woods toward the thing that is throwing trees. The road. We climb the fence till it stops.

Dog points the flashlight along the line of trunks. Their snapped ends are pale as bone, pointing the way to the mill, twenty miles off. Jim stares for a long time.

9.

JIM (CONT'D)

It's not a trap with a door, is it. It's a hall with one door open.

DOG

Yes.

JIM

(small)

I hate that you said yes.

Dog walks back down the road. At the path, the can swings on its wire. He steps onto the trail and it takes his weight the way it always has. He does not look back.

EXT. THE FOOTPATH – NIGHT

SUPER: 1:20 AM

Narrow. Spruce brushing both shoulders. Dog clicks the flashlight on for ten steps, off for ten, saving the batteries. Jim limps close behind, stepping on his heel.

When the flashlight is ON, we see the trail. When it clicks OFF, the screen goes fully black: only boots, breath, the drag of the tire iron. Each time it clicks on, something has changed. A can hangs where there was no can. A trunk leans an inch closer. We never see anything move. Only before and after.

JIM

Walk me through it. A man picks up a truck.

DOG

Not a man.

JIM

Great. Love that. So it throws trees. It builds a fence. Dog, a thing that builds a fence has plans.

DOG

Yes.

JIM

That's your whole answer?

DOG

Yes.

10.

JIM

(a hiccup of a laugh)

I want to go home.

DOG

(quietly)

Yes.

It is the kindest thing anyone has said to Jim all week.

A COFFEE CAN hangs from a branch at face height. A twist of gray tobacco in it. Twenty yards on, another. By forty, Jim stops flinching and starts counting. Cans. Cigarettes laid across stumps like tiny logs. A pint bottle with a dime in it. A work glove nailed palm-up to a trunk. A union pin. A cracked WRISTWATCH on a string, stopped at twenty to four.

JIM

It's a yard sale. It's the quietest yard sale in the world.

Nobody laughs. He just needed to hear a joke get made.

The cans begin to TINK softly. There is no wind. A ripple of small bright sounds moves through the trees, can to can, from behind them to ahead, as if something unseen is passing close along the path, brushing each one with a fingertip. It passes Jim. It passes Dog. It keeps going. Neither man turns around.

DOG

Mind the root.

Jim steps over it. He looks at Dog. Dog stepped over the same root a second earlier without looking down, like stairs in his own house.

JIM

You know this trail.

DOG

Some.

JIM

Some. Like somebody who walks it once a year. In, I don't know. July.

Dog keeps walking. Jim stops. Looks at Dog's back. At his right boot. At the pouch.

JIM (CONT'D)

Dog. Do you leave a toll?

11.

Dog doesn't turn. He nods, once.

JIM (CONT'D)

How long?

Dog raises both hands in the dark, in the flashlight beam. Nine fingers.

JIM (CONT'D)

Nine years? You've been tipping the forest for nine years and you let me stand there making jokes about it?

DOG

You were having fun.

Jim opens his mouth. Closes it. It's the nicest thing Dog has ever said to him, and it's on the worst night of his life.

EXT. THE PYRAMID – NIGHT

The path widens around a great flat stump. On it, a pyramid of rusted cans decades deep, a bottle at the top. A shrine, or a pile of something dead that is still being fed.

Jim stops. For six years the toll was a joke he told. He steps up to the trees like a man at a window.

JIM

I'd like to speak to whoever's in charge.

DOG

Jim.

JIM

I'm aware there's been a payment irregularity. I take full responsibility. I'd like to propose a payment plan.

His voice breaks. He keeps going. He pulls a limp, empty envelope from his shirt: TOLL on the flap.

JIM (CONT'D)

Net thirty, net sixty, whatever you want. I'm good for it. I'm not good for it. I will be. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry.

Silence. The woods listen.

12.

Then, from the dark, a single soft KNOCK. Not the two. The old one. Hello.

Relief goes through Jim so hard he nearly sits.

JIM (CONT'D)

It took the terms. I proposed a payment plan to a forest, and the forest said sure.

He reaches out, sobbing-happy, and pinches a twist of dry tobacco from the top can. Rolls it between his fingers.

JIM (CONT'D)

Just as collateral. They'll understand. It's practically theirs, it's just sitting here...

DOG

Don't.

JIM

It's an IOU. It's a swap...

DOG

Put it back.

Jim stuffs the pinch in his jacket pocket.

The woods go silent. Not quiet. Silent, the way a room goes after somebody says the wrong name at a funeral. One drop of water falls from a branch onto the tin of a can. The sound is enormous.

Dog is looking past him, at the black between two trunks to the left of the path.

DOG

Don't move.

From ten yards off the path, a VOICE. It does not sound like it comes from ten yards. It sits in the center of the mix, close to the ear, like a man leaning over a shoulder. Behind it: ice in a glass, a jukebox playing something we almost recognize, a bar on a Friday night.

VOICE (O.S.)

Everybody goes home richer.

It is Jim's voice. Not an imitation. Jim in the cab at five in the morning, three coffees in, delighted with himself. And under it, the little pleased laugh. He has said the line a hundred times and never heard how it sounds.

13.

It sounds like a man with his hand in somebody's pocket.

JIM

(soft)

Hey.

Because it's his voice. He knows that guy. That guy was great. That guy was never scared of anything.

VOICE (O.S.)

Everybody goes home richer.

Closer. Easy. Inviting. And behind it, a woman's laugh, a man's laugh, the clink of glasses. A place with lights. A place you could walk to.

Dog's flashlight clicks on. It lies across moss between the trunks: green, level, kind, like a lawn. Jim takes a step off the path.

His boot goes through the moss to the knee.

Cold closes on his shin. Black, old, bog stink. A fist closes on his collar and hauls. His leg comes out of the muck with a long wet suck, boot and all. He lands on his back on the trail.

DOG

Path.

The first time Jim has ever heard him say a word like a door he is holding shut.

JIM

(whisper)

Okay. Path.

The voice waits. Jim lies with his heart in his throat, leg wet to the thigh. The dark between the trees looks exactly as far away as a bar.

JIM (CONT'D)

It wasn't mocking me.

DOG

No.

JIM

It was practicing.

Dog doesn't answer. It is the answer.

14.

EXT. HOLLIS CAMP – NIGHT

SUPER: 2:15 AM

The trees come back before the camp does. A doorframe standing alone. A clearing, gray in the dusk. A cookhouse with a tree growing from its doorway. A bunkhouse with its roof fallen across its beds. At the far edge, a truck cab nose-down in the moss, no wheels, no glass. In the middle, one enormous white spruce.

SLOW LATERAL TRACK across the ruin. Each structure is framed through the gaps of another. Wood creaking, a long shift of weight in the cookhouse, as if the building is settling around someone who isn't there.

JIM

Fixer-upper. Great bones. Motivated sellers.

Nobody picks it up. Jim is shaking, a fine hard shiver. Dog takes a spare coat from his pack and holds it out.

JIM (CONT'D)

I'm not cold.

DOG

Put it on.

He does. The sleeves cover his knuckles. In the gray he looks about fourteen.

Dog crosses to the great spruce. Nine Julys he has passed this camp from the edge of the path and never gone closer than the width of the trail. The bark has been cut in columns. His thumb finds a notch at chest height, edges gone soft and gray. Beside it, a name burned into the wood so long ago the letters have healed like scars: HOLLIS. 1931.

He moves along. 1932. 1933. A notch for every July. PRUITT. 1959. PRUITT. 1963. PRUITT. 1973.

He looks up.

TILT UP, slow. Ten feet off the ground, where no ladder has ever stood, the bark has been cut a second time above every notch. Straight as a rule. A receipt. Someone has answered every one. Above the notches of the last nine years, beside the long cut, a small neat mark. Nine of them in a row.

He puts his palm flat on the bark under them. His hand shakes. He holds it there.

15.

JIM

(reading, lips moving)

That's a lot of marks.

Dog doesn't answer.

JIM (CONT'D)

Somebody's got a ladder.

DOG

There's no ladder.

Jim looks at Dog's hand on the bark. At the nine small cuts. Understands whose book it is.

JIM

It keeps your records.

DOG

Yes.

JIM

(gently)

Dog. That's the nicest thing a forest ever did and the creepiest.

EXT. THE OLD CAB – CONTINUOUS

Jim limps toward the wrecked cab on the slope, wanting only to be somewhere else. He steps onto the running board and stops.

JIM

Dog. There's a guy.

Behind the wheel, upright: a long-dead MAN. Canvas mackinaw. A felt hat still on the skull. Hands in his lap. Nothing broken. Nothing torn. A pocket watch hangs from the mirror on its chain, stopped at twenty to four. His boots are set neatly together. He looks composed, like a man who dressed for it.

JIM (CONT'D)

Mannequin. Hunting camp thing. They do this.

DOG

Look at the watch.

Jim looks. Twenty to four. Like the one on the string. He backs off the step, hand over his mouth.

16.

JIM

I'm going to sit. Just for a minute.

DOG

No.

Dog catches his arm. It comes out harder than he meant.

DOG (CONT'D)

He sat down. He waited. Every man who stopped on this path died at twenty to four.

JIM

That's not a rule. That's you making a rule.

DOG

Yes.

Jim looks at the watch again. Then at Dog's wrist. Dog's own watch ticks, loud now in the mix: 2:21.

JIM

It's two twenty.

DOG

We keep moving.

An AMBER LIGHT begins to glow on the dead man's dashboard. It brightens slowly, the way a tube set warms. There is no battery. The glow climbs the dead man's face from below, a plain, domestic, kitchen-at-night yellow, which is the most frightening thing on screen. A low hum. The tick of metal expanding as it heats.

A click. A rush of static, soft as rain on a roof. Then a man's voice, grainy and warm and impatient.

WALT (V.O.)

Jimmy? Jimmy, where the hell are you?

Jim goes still.

JIM

That's Walt.

DOG

No.

17.

WALT (V.O.)

If you're not on that scale by six I'm docking you the whole load. And I'm docking your mother.

Jim laughs, a high helpless bark. His whole body goes loose.

JIM

Dog. Listen to him. Nobody else does the mother thing.

DOG

Walt's at the yard. Twenty miles.

JIM

He drove out for the Hendricks thing.

DOG

Once.

JIM

Once is a precedent.

DOG

Ask him something.

Jim wets his lips and leans to the dial.

JIM

What did you short us in March?

WALT (V.O.)

Forty-one dollars and a scale discrepancy, and I'd do it again.

Jim's face crumples with joy.

JIM

It's him!

DOG

You told the whole yard.

JIM

(a beat)

I did tell the whole yard. I was upset.

WALT (V.O.)

(fatherly, warm as whiskey)

Jimmy. You leave the toll?

18.

The camp goes quiet.

WALT (V.O.) (CONT'D)

It's in your pocket. It weighs nothing. You spent it on the truck.

Jim's mouth opens. He does not look at Dog.

JIM

Course I've got it. Right here. Safe and sound.

WALT (V.O.)

Good boy. Then there's no problem. Come out the muskeg road. I've got the Dodge and both headlights on.

Through the broken wall of the bunkhouse, far off in the trees: TWO PALE LIGHTS, side by side, a car's width apart. They do not flicker.

JIM

Walt! We're coming! Hold the sandwich!

DOG

Jim...

Jim is already hopping, tire iron banging. Some small clear part of him knows it is wrong, the way you know a step is missing in the dark, and he goes anyway, because it is a light, and every light he's seen all night has been pointed at him.

EXT. THE X LINE – NIGHT

The lights are not headlights. They are SPHERES, each the size of a grapefruit, hanging a yard off the ground without a wire. Cold milk-white light. No beam. Jim sees his face in one, small and bent, like a face in a spoon.

JIM

Swamp gas. Methane. It's in a book.

Between the trunks every thirty yards: two saplings bent and lashed crosswise high between trees, forming an X. Another. Another. A line of them dwindling into the dark.

JIM (CONT'D)

Somebody's been doing crafts.

19.

WALT (O.S.)

Jimmy! What the hell are you doing with those lights? I'm over here! Come left!

So exactly Walt. The annoyance. The idiot. Jim steps off the trail past the last X. The lights dim to ash. The needles underfoot give a soft sigh.

WALT (O.S.) (CONT'D)

That's my boy. Right here.

JIM

Dog! I'm going left! Walt's on the left!

No answer. The milk-white light behind him has shrunk to two faint coins. A smell drifts in, sweet and sharp: wet dog and struck matches. Jim has never smelled that on Walt. Walt smells like diesel and Winstons.

He stops.

JIM (CONT'D)

(small)

Walt?

WALT (O.S.)

Keep going.

And then, softly, in another register, the voice laughs. It is Jim's own laugh. The high helpless bark, copied down to the last crack.

Jim turns to run. The ground gives.

It goes out from under him with a long wet sigh. Cold takes his legs, his hips, his chest. He flings out both arms and gets nothing: moss, water, black mud closing around his ribs like a fist. The tire iron slips from his hand and vanishes without a sound.

Far behind him, someone is shouting his name, over and over, ragged, getting no closer.

EXT. THE MUSKEG – NIGHT

SUPER: 2:40 AM

Dog bursts out of the spruce at a dead run and stops at the edge of a flat sheet of moss, green-gray, perfectly smooth. In the middle, a black hole the size of a tabletop. In the hole: Jim, to the chest, arms spread, mud to the chin.

20.

JIM

Hi. I'm in a swamp.

DOG

Don't move.

JIM

I wasn't planning on it. I'm going to die in a place with a moose name.

Dog goes down on his belly. He wraps the pack strap twice around his fist, sets the rifle on a root well back, and slides out across the quivering moss. Four feet. Eight. At twelve he throws the strap out.

DOG

Don't talk. Pull when I pull.

HANDHELD at moss level. The mat BREATHES, a slow swell under Dog's chest. We hold a long time on each pull. A crack races out under Dog's chin like a thin black vein. Jim's face at the bog's edge, only his eyes clear of the mud, milk-white light in each. Pull. Rest. Pull.

On the fifth pull the strap tears from the pack. Jim shoots backward onto the moss. The pack tips and goes down slowly, like something being lowered: two sandwiches, the thermos, the first-aid tin. The flashlight slides after it, throws its yellow beam up at the sky for one second, and the bog takes it.

Dog lies on his face, arms outflung. His left hand is still wrapped through the strap of the diesel can. He doesn't remember putting it there.

JIM

(on his back, black from the chin down)

For the record, I never liked that thermos.

Dog strips the soaked coat off Jim, wrings it, and wraps his own jacket around Jim's shoulders. He stands in his shirtsleeves and says nothing about the cold.

Two pale lights have come to the edge of the clearing, hanging exactly where the spruce meets the moss. They hold the margin like a flagman at a landing. Dog studies them. The lights flared when he stood inside the line of X's. They dimmed when Jim crossed the last one.

DOG

Stay in the light.

21.

JIM

Is that a rule you just made up?

DOG

Yes.

JIM

Good. It's got a nice ring.

Jim sits up. The grin lifts off his face all at once, as if someone reached in and took it. What is left is younger and a little ugly: a kid with his eyes too wide.

JIM (CONT'D)

Dog. I have to say something. And then you say something, and then we never talk about it again, and we're normal.

His black hands shake in front of him.

JIM (CONT'D)

I spent it. The envelope. Six hundred dollars. Monday I was two payments behind on the Kenworth, and the woman at the lender's, Deb, had the paper on the desk and a pen in her hand. I thought, I'll make it up on overtime. Nobody'll ever know. I put it on her desk and she said, oh, thank you, that really helps. And I walked out into the sunshine and thought, I'm the smartest man in Alaska.

His breath hitches.

JIM (CONT'D)

And last year I only left half. Three hundred. I told you it was handled.

DOG

I know.

Jim's head comes up.

JIM

What?

DOG

I saw it in June. In the yard. I saw you fold it. I saw you not look.

22.

JIM

And you didn't say anything?

DOG

No.

JIM

Why?

Dog turns the rifle over in his hands.

DOG

It wasn't mine to say.

Thirty men. Twenty dollars each. Walt wrote TOLL on it in capitals. I put it on a desk in town and a woman said thank you. Last year I left half. I said it was a bad month. It was never a bad month.

JIM (CONT'D)

(mouthed, subtitled)

I thought you were Pruitt's joke. You knocked and I knocked back to make fun of you. Every July. I thought it was funny. I'm sorry I made it a joke.

That is all. He has nothing behind it: no grin in front, no next sentence waiting. He sits on the gravel with the river roaring and his empty hands in his lap and lets the thing look at him.

Nobody moves. The generator throbs. A hundred watches tick.

From the trees, softly: a single knock.

It is a long time before Jim understands that it is an answer and not a greeting. The sound of a ledger being opened to a page.

43.

Dog goes down on his knees at the ash ring and moves fast, with a job in front of him. He digs a hole in the old ash with his hands. He takes the POUCH out of his boot, worn pale and soft as a glove, holds it against his chest for one second, and lays it in the center of the pit. He sets the notched stick beside it. He builds a small tight cone of bleached driftwood over both. He unscrews the cap of the diesel can and pours. He pours the last of the 1957 over it, and it runs down into the wood and shines.

He takes the tin from his pocket. Opens it. Small neat dark-headed matches, like a row of seeds. He strikes one.

It breaks. The head snaps off and falls on the gravel.

His hand is shaking again, like a leaf. Jim watches him see it.

DOG

(to his own hand)

Don't.

He strikes a second. It flares: a tiny orange tongue. He cups it and brings it down toward the wood, and the cold comes down off the trees.

The pressure, flat and slow, rolling off the dark like a tide. The flame leans. Shrinks. Goes blue and thin and out.

Dog kneels with a dead match between his fingers. He draws a third. His hand won't hold it. The head quivers against the box and will not strike. A low helpless sound comes out of him, the sound of someone who has run a very long way and found the door locked.

Jim drags himself across the gravel.

It takes a long time. He crosses twelve feet on his elbows and one good knee with the hole in his chest tearing at every pull. He comes up against Dog's shoulder and puts out both hands. They are black with blood and mud and shake like Dog's. He cups them around the match and around Dog's fingers, and holds on.

Dog looks down. He draws a long breath and strikes.

The match lights between their four hands: a tiny orange flower. The pressure comes down on them. The flame bends and shudders, and they hold it, the two of them, in a cave of fingers, in the dark and the cold and the noise of the river, until it is steady.

Dog puts it to the wood.

44.

The diesel takes with a soft WHUMP. Blue flame runs across the cone and up, and orange follows, and the light climbs the cairn, leaping from axe to axe, from watch to watch, a hundred little suns flashing and swinging on their chains. Smoke goes up in a thick sweet column and bends toward the trees.

At the edge of the clearing, in the glow of the toll fire, the nine-foot shape takes one slow step forward onto the gravel.

EXT. THE FORD – THE TOLL FIRE – DAWN

It stands at the edge of the firelight, upright, a little bent with age. Gray hair, long and matted with burrs. Shoulders wide as a doorway. Arms hanging to its knees, hands open at its sides, palms forward, empty, held out as for change. A broad, old, tired face, a pale scar across the brow. Dark wet clear eyes: the eyes of someone who has waited a very long time at a counter and never once raised his voice.

The first time we see it whole. One wide frame, held, no cutting. The fire throws its shadow nine feet high across the cairn and a thousand watches, and they flash in it like eyes. We are finally allowed to look. It does not look at us. It looks at Dog.

It is not terrible. It is only very large, and old, and waiting for him to finish.

Dog kneels. He wants to say a clear thing first, to set down in front of it like a plate. His mouth is dry.

DOG

In sixty-six I fired at you from the door of a bunkhouse. You'd done nothing. You'd stopped to look at me. I was afraid, and I fired.

His voice cracks. He begins again.

45.

DOG (CONT'D)

I've paid every year since and I never said I was sorry. I thought the tobacco said it for me. Tonight I fired again. I don't know where I aimed. He was in front of me. He's all I've got. I shot him. I took a man's whiskey off a shelf to wash it. And I watched him pocket the toll in June and said nothing, because I wanted somebody else to owe.

Behind him in the dark, Jim's breath, rough and small.

DOG (CONT'D)

That's all of it. I haven't left anything out.

He reaches into the fire.

He does not think. If he thought, he would not do it. Both hands into the flames to the wrists. He closes them on the pouch and lifts it out.

It is a burning coal in his hands. The sound that comes out of him is not a word. He does not drop it. He holds it before his chest as a boy holds a bird, smoke rolling into his face, tobacco hissing and popping in the burning leather.

DO NOT SHOW THE HANDS. Stay on Dog's face, extreme close. The fire in one eye. In the other, reflected small and tall, the dark shape of the creature, watching. We understand what is happening from his face, the hiss of the leather, and the sound Jim cannot make.

A long time passes. The pouch shrinks and crumbles and goes to a handful of glowing ash that sifts down between his fingers. There is nothing in his hands but heat and the dull awful red of what is left of his palms.

He opens them.

The wind comes off the river and takes the last of the smoke across the gravel and out over the water.

The creature steps into the circle of the fire. It stops in front of him, close enough that he can smell it, and it doesn't smell of wet dog or struck matches. It smells of the woods in the first hour of rain.

46.

It looks at his hands. It lifts one of its own: enormous, gray, thick-fingered, cracked at the knuckles like his own. It reaches out slowly and touches, with one finger, the black ruin of his palm.

It does not hurt. He thought it would. The touch is light and dry. Then it is gone, and the creature's finger is gray with ash.

It looks at the ash. Then at the fire, where the notched stick lies in the coals, the notches glowing.

It nods once.

It crosses the shingle to the flat stone, stoops, and takes up the oilcloth bundle in one hand. It walks to the rock where Jim lies propped. It kneels, that size and that age, in the gravel, in the light of a fire. It lays the oilcloth in Jim's lap and draws back its hand.

Jim stares down. He stares up. His face is a ruin of tears and blood and astonishment, and a hoarse broken sound comes out of him that Dog knows at once, because he has heard it a thousand times and never once at the right moment.

A laugh.

The creature rises. It steps back from the fire and crosses to the edge of the water and stands on the last of the stones with the river going by. It turns its two open hands toward the far bank. It holds them there.

It is not a threat. It is a door.

DOG

(raw)

It'll let us go.

He lifts his hands and the world goes white, and he sets them down again and rocks, teeth locked. Jim is dragging himself toward him across the stones. Dog shakes his head.

DOG (CONT'D)

Don't. I've got you.

He gets his forearms under Jim's knees, Jim's arms around his neck, Jim's weight onto his back. He stands. It takes everything he has. He walks past the nine-foot shape without looking at it, and it lets him, and steps aside by exactly the width of one man.

47.

EXT. THE RIVER – DAWN

Cold enough to stop the heart. It closes around his boots, his shins, his knees. The current takes hold and leans. Round stones roll underfoot. At his thighs the river is a muscle. His burned hands are held out before him like a pair of dead things.

Jim's grip slips. His arms have no strength left. The wet cold hands loosen at Dog's throat.

Dog has no hands.

He turns his head. Bends his neck. Takes the cuff of Jim's sleeve, where it hangs over his shoulder, between his teeth, and clamps down.

They go on that way, an absurd, ugly, undignified thing: somebody carrying somebody across a river by the sleeve, like a dog with a pup. The water rises to his chest. The lights on the far bank come nearer. Over the roar of the river, a long way off, a BELL begins to ring. The bell we have been waiting for since the first frame.

LONG LENS from the far bank: two small heads in a vast moving river, one carrying the other, against the pearl sky.

A tiny dry regular sound reaches Dog from his own wrist, where his forearm hooks Jim's knee. It takes him a long moment to know it.

His watch. It is ticking again.

EXT. THE FAR BANK – SUNRISE

SUPER: 4:44 AM

The sun comes up behind the trees on the side of the river they left. No gold in it: a thin pale seam along the top of the black spruce, going from pearl to the inside of a shell. Jim lies on his back on the gravel with the collar of Dog's jacket turned up against his cheek.

Across the river, the toll fire has burned down to a thread of smoke. Behind it the trees are only trees.

Dog sits beside him, knees up, hands held out on his knees, palms up, wrapped in strips of his own shirt, dark and wet. A little smoke still comes off the cloth.

DOG

I'll never hold a saw again.

48.

He says it to the river. Not a complaint. The way you would say the time. Jim looks at the hands, and at the face above them, and shapes the words without breath.

JIM

(mouthed, subtitled)

I'm sorry.

DOG

I'm not.

Jim looks at him a long time in the thin light. It is the first time in six years he has seen Dog's face with nothing on it. Lined, burned, ten years older than at midnight, and at ease.

Jim opens the oilcloth in his lap with fingers that won't close. Fifteen twenty-dollar bills, green with mold, curled. He takes out his wallet and counts the eleven and the forty. Three hundred and fifty-one dollars. He does the other arithmetic. Six hundred, to thirty men. A hole in his chest. A Kenworth on its side in a ditch, a payment due Friday. No voice.

He looks at it. And what rises in him is a laugh. A dry hiss. It hurts. He lies on the gravel shaking with it, mouth open, no sound at all, tears running into his ears, and Dog turns and looks at him with deep concern.

Jim holds up the three hundred in his shaking hand. Points at it. Points at the river. And shapes one word with his lips, so there can be no mistake:

JIM

(mouthed, subtitled)

Interest.

Dog stares. His face comes apart slowly from the middle, like ice going out of a river. A sound comes out of him Jim has never heard in six years: a low rough cracked noise from below the ribs. He laughs. He bends over his ruined hands and laughs until he coughs, and cannot stop.

Something touches Jim's wrist. A tickle. A sting.

On the back of his hand, in the first light, sits a MOSQUITO.

A big dull gray July mosquito with long legs and a delicate, hungry, absurd attention. It sinks its needle into his skin and drinks. The first living thing he has seen all night other than the two of them and the one in the trees.

49.

SOUND: the mosquito's whine, thin and perfect, is the first insect we have heard since the June yard. It is the loveliest sound in the film. Under it the world comes back, layer by layer: a breath of wind in the spruce, the generator, a screen door, a bird.

DOG

(wiping his eyes with his wrist)

You got your mosquito.

Jim doesn't swat it. He lies with his hand held out in the light and lets it take what it wants.

Up the slope, a screen door SLAMS. Boots on gravel, coming. Somebody shouting for the medic. A DOG BARKING: an actual dog. The best sound Jim has ever heard.

Then, from across the river: a knock.

Just one. Soft and round and clear. Wood on wood from the edge of the stones. The oldest sound Jim knows, the first anyone taught him on this grade, and for six years he answered it with a grin.

He looks. At the far water's edge, in the thin smoke, a tall gray shape stands upright, far away, only a darker patch against the gray. Quite still, hands at its sides.

His hand moves before he can stop it. An old reflex, six years deep: knuckles drawn back, the grin. He has his fist half raised to knock. He can see how it would go. The cocky double rap. Hey, forest, no hard feelings.

Dog's burned hand comes down over his wrist. It is gentle. It does not close.

Jim looks at him.

Dog shakes his head. Then he turns and looks across the water and gets up, in stages, like an old man, hands held out in front of him. He walks three steps to a driftwood log half out of the gravel at the water's edge, gray and dry and long as a canoe. He bends. He sets one bandaged elbow on the wood.

KNOCK. KNOCK.

Two. Slow, spaced, deliberate. A count, not a greeting. A man who has been given a bill and has paid it, saying so.

It carries a long way over the water, comes back off the far trees, and dies.

50.

Dog straightens. He stands with his back to Jim, looking across at the gray shape in the smoke. When he speaks it is not loud. It is the plain, rough, ordinary voice of someone who spent nine years learning how to say nothing and has only now found out what it was for.

DOG

Thank you.

The shape stands a long time. The river goes by between them, bright now, almost gold. Then it goes down on all fours. It turns, easily, and moves into the trees without a sound, and the morning closes behind it.

Boots hit the gravel. A flashlight. A loud kind voice: jesus, jesus Christ, hold on, buddy, hold on. A man's face above Jim in a hard hat, enormous, sunburned, wet-eyed with astonishment. HANDS lift him. He lets them. He has a mosquito on his hand and the sky in his eyes and Dog's name safe behind his teeth.

Dog stands over them, hands held out in front of him, as the medics reach for him too. He lets them.

Jim, on the stretcher, catches Dog's eye. He lifts his eyebrows. A question. The old one. He holds up fingers: a number on a scale.

Dog looks at him. Thinks about it.

DOG

Four.

Jim opens his mouth for the joke. Nothing comes. He finds he doesn't need one. He lies in the arms of strangers with three hundred and fifty-one dollars in his fist and a hole in his chest and the whole bright morning going by overhead. His lips move, with great peace, shaping the last thing on his mind:

JIM

(mouthed, subtitled)

Still due Friday.

Dog, across the gravel, reads it. Almost smiles.

A mosquito whines. The river runs gold. Somewhere across it, a very long way off, wood knocks softly on wood. Once.

Jim lets it be.
